# From $1 to 2.71828: The Discovery of e

In mathematics, one of the most important numbers is e which is called Euler's number, and is approaximately equal to 2.71828. This number can be found in many places like, population growth, radioactive decay, and many formulas in calculus.

But unlike π, which comes from measuring circles, the number e has a very practical origin. It came from thinking about money in a bank account.

The idea was developed by the Swiss mathematician Jacob Bernoulli in the late 1600s. He was trying to understand a simple problem about compound interest.

Imagine you put $1 in a bank account that gives 100% interest per year. If the interest is added only once at the end of the year, your money simply doubles.After one year it's value becomes $2. But Bernoulli noticed something very interesting. What if the bank adds interest more often?

Interest can be added many times during the year. Each time it is added, the new total also starts earning interest. This is called compound interest.

Bernoulli calculated what happens when the interest is added more and more frequently.

Once a year: $2.000000

Twice a year: $2.25

Four times a year: $2.441406

Monthly: about $2.61

Weekly: about $2.69

Daily: about $2.7146

So the money keeps growing, but each improvement becomes smaller. For example, moving from weekly to daily interest adds only a few cents. Bernoulli then observed something more extreme. What if the bank added interest every hour? Or every minute? Or even every second?

As the compounding happens more and more often, the value gets closer and closer to a special number: 2.718281828…

It never goes past this value, but it keeps getting closer to it. This number is the famous constant e.

If $1 grows with 100% interest for one year under continuous compounding, the final amount becomes exactly:

≈ $2.71828

So the number e was not discovered while studying shapes or geometry. It came from a very practical question related to money.

From this simple idea, mathematicians discovered a number that later became one of the most important constants in mathematics and science.

Today, e appears in many fields—from finance to physics. Yet its story begins with something very ordinary: a single dollar in a bank account, quietly earning interest.
