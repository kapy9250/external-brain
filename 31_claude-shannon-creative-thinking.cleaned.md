# Claude Shannon: Creative Thinking

This document is a record of a speech on "Creative Thinking" given by Claude Shannon at Bell Labs on March 20, 1952.

*Translated from the provided Chinese transcript.*

A very small percentage of the population produces a large proportion of the important ideas. This is similar to a point made by the British mathematician Turing: the human brain is like a piece of uranium. If the human brain is below the critical point, you shoot a neutron into it, and the collision produces more additional neutrons. This leads to an extremely explosive growth. Turing said this is like ideas in the human brain.

There are some people who, if you shoot an idea into their brain, you only get half an idea back. Others are past that point, and for every idea you feed in, they produce two. These are the people past the "knee of the curve."

I don't think I'm past the knee of this curve, but I do know people who have been. Isaac Newton, for example, was solidly at the top of this curve. At 25, his output in science, physics, and mathematics was enough to make 10 to 20 people famous. He discovered the binomial theorem, calculus, the law of universal gravitation, the laws of motion, the decomposition of white light, and more.

What makes a person reach this part of the curve? What are the basic requirements? I think there are three necessary conditions for scientific research, invention, mathematics, physics, or similar fields.

*   **First is obvious: training and experience.** You can't expect a lawyer, no matter how smart, to give you a new theory of physics.
*   **Second is quantitative intelligence or talent.** You must have a fairly high IQ to do excellent scientific work. This might be a matter of genetics.
*   **The third component is Motivation.** The first two are not enough. Motivation is what makes an Einstein or a Newton. It's the drive, the desire to find answers, to find out how things work. Without it, you won't ask questions or find answers, regardless of your training or intelligence.

Motivation is hard to pin down. It might relate to personality, early training, and childhood experiences. At a surface level, a good scientist has a lot of **curiosity**. They want to know the answers to how things work.

Then there's the idea of **constructive dissatisfaction**. This is expressed as: "This is okay, but I think it can be done better. There's a cleaner way." It's a slight, persistent irritation when things don't look quite right.

Another thing is the **joy** of seeing the final result, the method, the engineering design, or the equipment. The thrill of proving a theorem or finding an elegant solution to an engineering problem.

Assuming someone has these three attributes, are there tricks or techniques that can aid creative work? I think there are. Good research workers apply them unconsciously, but you can also apply them consciously.

### 1. Simplification
When you get a problem, a very powerful method is to try to eliminate everything from it except the essential elements; chop it down to size. If you can boil the problem down to its essence, you might find a solution to the simplified version, and then add refinements back until you solve the original problem.

### 2. Seeking Similar Known Problems
You have a problem P, and somewhere there is a solution S. If you have experience, you might know a somewhat similar problem P' that has a known solution S'. You just need to find the analogy from P' to P, and from S' to S. That's why experience is so important. Two small jumps are easier than one big jump.

### 3. Restate the Problem from Multiple Angles
Try to restate the problem in as many different forms as possible. Change the wording, change the perspective. If you don't, it's easy to get stuck in mental ruts. That's why someone completely new to a problem can sometimes find a solution immediately after you've struggled for months.

### 4. Generalization
Once you find an answer to something, the next thing is to ask yourself if you can generalize it. If it was in two dimensions, do it in n dimensions. If someone has a clever way of doing things, ask: "Can I apply the same principle more generally to a larger class of problems?"

### 5. Structural Analysis
If the jump from problem to solution is too big, break it down into many small jumps. Set up subsidiary theorems or propositions. Once you prove those, you can reach the final solution. In design, you might first build a clumsy, bulky method, and once you have something that works, you start cutting away the unnecessary components.

### 6. Inversion
If you are trying to get from premise P to solution S and can't, turn the problem around. Assume S is given, and you are trying to get to P. You might find a direct route in that direction, and then you can reverse the steps in small batches.

Claude Shannon, Bell Labs
March 20, 1952
